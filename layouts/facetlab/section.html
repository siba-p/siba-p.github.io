What do the site types and GCN mean?

An atom's coordination number {{ transform.ToMath `\mathrm{CN}` $I }} counts its nearest neighbours: 12 in the bulk, 9 on a {111} terrace, 8 on a {100} terrace, 7 on edges and 6 or fewer on vertices. The generalized coordination number also weights each neighbour by how coordinated it is itself,

{{ transform.ToMath `\overline{\mathrm{CN}}_i = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{nn}(i)} \frac{\mathrm{CN}_j}{\mathrm{CN}_{\max}}, \qquad \mathrm{CN}_{\max} = 12,` $D }}

and tracks adsorption energies on metal surfaces almost linearly, which is why it is used to rank catalytic sites (Calle-Vallejo et al., 2015). Infinite terraces have {{ transform.ToMath `\overline{\mathrm{CN}} = 7.5` $I }} on {111} and {{ transform.ToMath `6.67` $I }} on {100}.

Why does size matter?

Below about 3 nm, most atoms are on the surface and a large share of them sit on edges and vertices. These low-coordinated sites dominate catalysis, ligand binding and growth. The curves show how the dispersion (surface fraction) and the edge-plus-vertex share fall as the particle grows, for the selected shape and metal.

About FacetLab

Gold and other FCC nanoparticles are rarely spheres. Their facets, edges and vertices decide where ligands, citrate or reactants bind. FacetLab builds the exact lattice structure for the common shapes, classifies every surface atom, and exports coordinates ready for a simulation.

Python package and command line: pip install git+https://github.com/siba-p/nanomatkit, then nanomatkit np --shape truncated_octahedron -k 2 -o Au201.xyz.

Method

Construction. FCC sites {{ transform.ToMath `\mathbf{r} = \tfrac{a}{2}(i,j,k)` $I }} with {{ transform.ToMath `i+j+k` $I }} even, kept if

{{ transform.ToMath `|x|+|y|+|z| \le S \quad \{111\}, \qquad \max(|x|,|y|,|z|) \le M \quad \{100\}.` $D }}

Wulff shape: plane distances scale with surface energy,

{{ transform.ToMath `\frac{M}{S/\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\gamma_{100}}{\gamma_{111}}.` $D }}

Equivalent diameter from the FCC volume per atom {{ transform.ToMath `a^3/4` $I }}:

{{ transform.ToMath `d = \left(\frac{6}{\pi}\,\frac{N a^3}{4}\right)^{1/3}.` $D }}

Ligands: {{ transform.ToMath `N_\mathrm{lig} = \sigma_g A` $I }}, with {{ transform.ToMath `A` $I }} summed over surface atoms ({{ transform.ToMath `\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}a^2` $I }} per {111}-like site, {{ transform.ToMath `\tfrac{a^2}{2}` $I }} per {100} site).

Checks: magic numbers 13, 55, 147, 309 (cuboctahedra) and 38, 201, 586, 1289 (truncated octahedra); {{ transform.ToMath `\mathrm{Au}_{201}` $I }} has 122 surface atoms.

  2. L. D. Marks & L. Peng, J. Phys.: Condens. Matter 28, 053001 (2016) (nanoparticle shape).
